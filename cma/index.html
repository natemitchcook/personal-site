Start with the work, not the AI

What are we trying to solve?

CMA's public site describes preconstruction estimating, budgeting, buyouts, value engineering, cost forecasting, billings and change orders. The opportunity is to understand where those handoffs break down and whether better data, existing software, or a focused assistant would make a measurable difference.

01 / ACCURACY

Catch what an estimate misses

Missing scope, outdated unit prices, incorrect quantities, unclear assumptions, or subcontractor bid gaps.

02 / FEEDBACK

Learn from completed jobs

Connect estimates to committed and actual costs, then feed recurring variances into future bids.

03 / VISIBILITY

See margin risk earlier

Bring together budgets, commitments, pending changes, forecast-to-complete and accounting records.

04 / TIME

Cut repetitive work

Reduce manual drawing takeoffs, duplicate data entry, invoice matching, and chasing project documents.

Conversation guide

Questions worth asking

Start with a real project and ask people to show how the work happens. Open each section to see the questions and the evidence to request.

1. Where does the money go?

When a project finishes below its expected margin, what usually caused it? How much comes from estimating omissions versus execution, design changes, or market conditions? Which contract types put CMA's own margin at risk? Who owns the final cost exposure?

Ask for: anonymized estimate-to-final-cost variance on 3–5 completed jobs, by cost code and root cause.

2. How does an estimate get made and approved?

Where do drawings, specs, quantities, subcontractor quotes, allowances, contingencies and labor rates come from? Which steps happen in PDFs, Excel or email? How are scope exclusions documented? Who checks assumptions before a bid goes out? How are revisions compared?

Ask for: one sanitized bid package, estimate workbook, bid leveling sheet, and approval checklist.

3. What can you learn from past projects?

Can estimators search actual costs from similar buildings and trades? Are cost codes consistent between estimating, procurement, project controls and accounting? Is historical pricing normalized for date, location and scope? Do teams conduct post-job reviews?

Ask for: historical cost database structure and one example of a lessons-learned review.

4. How do costs move from field to finance?

Which systems track budgets, purchase orders, subcontracts, invoices, RFIs, change events, change orders, WIP and forecasts? What gets re-entered? How frequently do project managers and accounting reconcile? How quickly are pending changes reflected in forecasts?

Ask for: anonymized cost report, WIP report, cost-code chart and system map.

5. What slows the accounting team down?

Where do AP coding, invoice approvals, lien waivers, pay applications, retention, reconciliations and month-end close stall? Which errors recur? Are approvals tied to commitments and actual progress?

Ask for: a redacted invoice-to-payment workflow, exception queue, and month-end close checklist.

6. What tools, data and constraints already exist?

What estimating, takeoff, BIM, project management, document storage and ERP tools are in use? What licenses and integrations are available? Who owns the data? Are there contractual confidentiality, client, security, audit or AI-use restrictions?

Ask for: application inventory, data access permissions, integration documentation and security requirements.

7. What would success look like in 90 days?

Would CMA value fewer estimate omissions, shorter bid cycles, more bids per estimator, earlier margin alerts, faster invoice processing or quicker close? What baseline exists? Who would sponsor a pilot and review its accuracy?

Ask for: baseline KPIs, one accountable process owner and a realistic pilot dataset.

Practical first experiment

Pick one costly workflow. Prove it works.

A sensible starting point is a retrospective test on completed projects, without touching live financial approvals.

WEEK 1–2

Map and measure

Interview an estimator, project manager, controller and executive sponsor. Trace one job from bid to final cost. Establish error and cycle-time baselines.

WEEK 3–4

Run a controlled comparison

Test an existing estimating tool or read-only AI reviewer against past bid packages. Record missed scope, false positives, time saved and reviewer corrections.

WEEK 5–6

Evaluate the business case

Compare results with the current workflow. Calculate avoided review hours and potential cost exposure, not hypothetical guaranteed savings.

DECISION

Buy, integrate, build or stop

Prefer the current platform when it already solves the problem. Build custom agents only when the workflow or data advantage is genuinely unique.

Minimum safeguards
  • Use approved, redacted documents and explicit data permissions.
  • Keep estimates, invoices and payments subject to qualified human approval.
  • Track source citations, audit logs, exceptions, and false-positive/false-negative rates.
  • Compare performance across project types, not just one successful demonstration.
What other contractors have tried

Real examples, useful lessons

These are published customer stories, often from vendors. They illustrate actual implementations, but their reported outcomes are not independent proof of what CMA would achieve.

DPR Construction + Autodesk Takeoff

Piloted connected 2D/3D quantity takeoff on a $100M life sciences project. Autodesk reports that drawing organization saved roughly 3–4 hours of setup across a 500+ drawing package.

Lesson: Test against one complex project before a wider rollout.

Read customer story

Suffolk Construction + Speckle

Structured design and BIM data, mapped it to standardized cost codes and fed it into estimation systems, reducing manual preparation.

Lesson: Consistent cost codes and clean data may matter more than a chatbot.

Read customer story

Buffalo Construction + O3XO

Built an estimator's specification assistant, closeout automation and proposal workflow around existing Procore, Sage and SharePoint systems.

Lesson: Map work with estimating, accounting and operations before deciding what to build.

Read customer story

Coastal Construction + Togal.AI

Adopted AI-assisted quantity takeoff to reduce manual plan measurement. Vendor-reported results include significant estimator time savings.

Lesson: Quantity takeoff is a testable entry point, but speed alone does not prove better margin.
